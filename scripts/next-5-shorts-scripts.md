# Next 5 Shorts: scripts and shot lists

Channel: **Unreal But True**. Post them in this order, about one a day. Each voiceover is about 80 to 95 words, so roughly 30 to 35 seconds at a natural pace. The closing lines come from [closing-subscribe-lines.md](closing-subscribe-lines.md).

How to build each one:
- **First 2 seconds:** the strongest visual and the hook line together, with no intro or logo.
- **Captions:** big, centred and burned in, with one key word per line highlighted.
- **Shots:** change every 2 to 3 seconds. Use stock footage (Pexels, Pixabay, NASA, Wikimedia Commons) or AI clips from the prompts below.
- **Title:** plain words, no emoji, no hashtags in the title. Put #shorts in the description.

## 1. Krakatoa: The Loudest Sound in History

**Title:** The Loudest Sound in History
**Description:** In 1883 a volcano exploded so loudly it was heard 3,000 miles away. #shorts #science #history

| Time | Voiceover | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3s | "This sound was heard 3,000 miles away." | Black screen, then a volcano explosion. Caption: **3,000 MILES** |
| 3–9s | "August 27th, 1883. The volcano Krakatoa, in Indonesia, blew itself apart." | Map zooming in on Krakatoa, between Java and Sumatra |
| 9–15s | "On an island near Africa, people heard what sounded like distant cannon fire. It came from the other side of the ocean." | Map line from Krakatoa to Rodrigues Island |
| 15–21s | "Sailors on a ship 40 miles away had their eardrums burst." | Old sailing ship in rough sea. Caption: **40 MILES AWAY** |
| 21–28s | "The pressure wave was so strong that barometers around the world recorded it passing seven times, over five days." | Globe with a ring expanding around it, repeating |
| 28–33s | "Heard 3,000 miles away. Unreal, but true. Subscribe." | Ash cloud. Caption: **SUBSCRIBE** |

**AI clip prompt (opening):** "Vertical 9:16, a massive volcanic island exploding at dawn, giant ash column and shockwave rippling across the ocean, 19th-century sailing ship in the foreground, cinematic, realistic, dramatic light."

**Fact sources:** Simon Winchester, *Krakatoa: The Day the World Exploded*. The Royal Society's 1888 report on the eruption, which records the sound on Rodrigues Island and the pressure wave readings. Avoid quoting a decibel figure, because the estimates vary.

## 2. Lake Natron: Animals Don't Turn to Stone

**Title:** The Lake That "Turns Animals to Stone" (The Real Story)
**Description:** The viral photos are real, but the myth isn't. #shorts #science #nature

| Time | Voiceover | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3s | "You've seen this lake turn animals to stone. It doesn't." | Calcified bird on a rock. Caption: **MYTH** |

| 9–16s | "Birds that die here get soaked in a natural salt called natron. The same salt ancient Egyptians used to make mummies." | Egyptian mummy, then lake shore |
| 16–23s | "So the bodies don't rot. They dry out and harden, and look like statues." | Calcified animals. Caption: **PRESERVED, NOT STONE** |
| 23–28s | "The famous photos? A photographer found the bodies on the shore and posed them." | Camera, then posed bird on a branch |
| 28–33s | "Not stone, but preserved like statues. Unreal, but true. Subscribe." | Flamingos on the lake. Caption: **SUBSCRIBE** |

**AI clip prompt (B-roll):** "Vertical 9:16, aerial drone shot of a blood-red salt lake in East Africa, white salt crusts, flocks of pink flamingos, volcano in the background, realistic."

**Fact sources:** Nick Brandt's 2013 photo series and his interviews, where he says he found and posed the bodies. National Geographic coverage of Lake Natron. Use real calcified-animal photos only if you have the rights. Otherwise use AI or illustration.

**Description:** About 4 km down, the rock is hot enough to burn you. #shorts #science #earth

| Time | Voiceover | On screen |

## 5. Death Valley: The Rocks That Move by Themselves

**Title:** The Rocks That Move by Themselves
**Description:** For decades nobody saw them move. Then scientists filmed it. #shorts #science #nature

| Time | Voiceover | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3s | "These rocks move across the desert on their own." | Rock with a long trail behind it. Caption: **ON THEIR OWN** |
| 3–9s | "Racetrack Playa, Death Valley. Some weigh as much as a person, and leave trails hundreds of metres long." | Wide shot of the dry lakebed with many trails |
| 9–15s | "For decades, nobody ever saw one move. Theories ranged from strong winds to magnetism." | Old photos. Caption: **NOBODY SAW IT** |
| 15–24s | "Then in 2014, scientists put GPS trackers on rocks and filmed the answer. After rain, a thin sheet of ice forms. When it melts and breaks up, a light wind pushes the ice, and the ice pushes the rocks." | Time-lapse, thin ice panes moving, rocks sliding |
| 24–29s | "Slowly. A few metres a minute." | Close-up of rock moving. Caption: **A FEW METRES A MINUTE** |
| 29–34s | "Moved by ice and wind, not magic. Unreal, but true. Subscribe." | Sunset over the playa. Caption: **SUBSCRIBE** |

**AI clip prompt (opening):** "Vertical 9:16, flat cracked desert lakebed at sunrise, a single rock with a long curved trail behind it, mountains in the background, realistic, wide angle, slow push-in."

**Fact sources:** Norris et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2014, "Sliding Rocks on Racetrack Playa, Death Valley National Park". National Park Service page on Racetrack Playa.
